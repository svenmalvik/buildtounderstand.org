# History and precedent: Where Should the Platform End?

## Long arc

The boundary problem predates both “platform engineering” and AI. A service becomes platform-like when one group turns a repeated capability into an interface that other groups can consume without negotiating every implementation detail. Amazon EC2’s 2006-08-24 beta is an early public example: AWS owned physical capacity, virtualization, metering, and the service interface, while customers retained control of their machine images and applications. The interface removed provisioning work without requiring AWS to own customer software ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2006/08/24/announcing-amazon-elastic-compute-cloud-amazon-ec2---beta/)). Heroku’s Twelve-Factor App later showed the complementary, contract-shaped approach: publish conventions that make applications portable across execution environments rather than own every part of their lifecycle ([Twelve-Factor App](https://www.12factor.net/)).

DevOps shifted responsibility toward teams that build and run their services, but the resulting cloud, delivery, security, and observability toolchains created their own cognitive load. The modern platform response is therefore not simply “centralize operations again.” It is to offer self-service capabilities and paved or golden paths as products for internal developers. Spotify’s backend Golden Path began as guidance and a recommended workflow; engineers could leave it, but then accepted less platform support. Spotify later coupled those recommendations to automation while still describing teams as autonomous ([Spotify Golden Paths](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2020/08/how-we-use-golden-paths-to-solve-fragmentation-in-our-software-ecosystem), [Spotify DevOps productivity](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2020/8/how-we-improved-developer-productivity-for-our-devops-teams)). Team Topologies calls the smallest useful version a Thinnest Viable Platform and explicitly allows it to be only documentation; Thoughtworks similarly recommends an incremental sequence from documentation to templates and pull requests, then self-service APIs only when evidence warrants them ([Team Topologies](https://teamtopologies.com/key-concepts-content/what-is-a-thinnest-viable-platform-tvp), [Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques/incremental-developer-platform)).

Machine learning made the boundary harder because the model is only one part of a production system. Google’s technical-debt paper identifies data dependencies, glue code, configuration, serving, monitoring, and external-world changes as sources of system-level debt around ML code ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/hidden-technical-debt-in-machine-learning-systems/)). Large organizations consequently built broad platforms: Uber’s Michelangelo covered data, training, evaluation, deployment, prediction, and monitoring, while Google’s TFX supported continuous production pipelines ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/gb/en/blog/michelangelo-machine-learning-platform/), [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/opml19/presentation/baylor)). Those examples establish that integrated platforms can repay their cost at large scale; they do not establish that the same ownership bundle is universal. A systematic mapping study published on 2024-06-28 found 35 MLOps architecture components and multiple architecture variants across 43 primary studies, evidence that MLOps is an architectural design space rather than one inevitable product boundary ([Najafabadi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19847)).

The platform-engineering movement made the organizational contract explicit: platform teams treat internal developers as customers and provide consistent capabilities without absorbing product accountability. The CNCF Platforms Working Group described platforms as a way to reuse infrastructure and application capabilities while enabling DevOps-style autonomy, not as a fixed feature list ([CNCF](https://www.cncf.io/blog/2023/04/11/announcing-a-white-paper-on-platforms-for-cloud-native-computing/)). Google’s current definition likewise says an internal developer platform may or may not include a portal and that there is no one-size-fits-all design ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/solutions/platform-engineering)). DORA’s Accelerate State of DevOps research complicates the sales pitch: internal-platform use was associated with higher perceived productivity and performance but also lower change stability and throughput, so independence and outcomes matter alongside adoption ([DORA](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/)).

Catalogues and integration standards illustrate where a platform can stop short of runtime control. Backstage centralizes and renders metadata, but its documentation says source-controlled descriptors or external authoritative systems feed the catalogue, and that the catalogue should be treated as a cache rather than the ultimate source of truth for dynamic facts ([Backstage catalogue](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/), [Backstage catalogue graph](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/creating-the-catalog-graph/)). Kubernetes followed a similar pattern at a lower layer: it replaced bundled, vendor-specific integrations with interfaces such as the Container Runtime Interface and external cloud-controller managers, allowing implementers to evolve independently from the core project ([Kubernetes dockershim history](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2022/05/03/dockershim-historical-context/), [Kubernetes cloud providers](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2023/12/14/cloud-provider-integration-changes/)). These precedents support a precise historical lesson: defining an interface and operating every implementation are separate commitments.

LLM agents add new pressure without erasing that history. MCP, introduced on 2024-11-25, standardizes connections between AI applications and external systems, but its architecture assigns orchestration, consent, connection permissions, and authorization decisions to the host while servers expose focused resources, prompts, and tools ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/model-context-protocol), [MCP architecture](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/architecture/index)). MCP authorization protects HTTP transport and resource access, but it does not make a catalogue entry or tool description equivalent to business authorization ([MCP authorization](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/basic/authorization)). AI therefore sharpens the old boundary question: the shared platform can standardize discovery and transport while identity, data entitlement, tool authority, acceptable outcomes, and runtime accountability still sit at several different decision points.

Agent-specific adapters and metadata can extend an existing capability while runtime, review, and product accountability remain separate. |

## Failed attempts at the same thing

The public record does not support calling every retired platform a failure. The more defensible category is **boundary choices that became too coupled, too broad, economically unjustified, or outgrown**. Four well-documented cases meet that test.

### 1. Kubernetes owning a Docker-specific adapter in core

Early Kubernetes supported Docker Engine directly. When multiple runtimes emerged, the project created CRI but kept `dockershim` in the kubelet because Docker Engine did not implement that interface. Kubernetes later said the special-case code added complexity, produced inconsistent integrations, and became a heavy maintainer burden; it was removed on 2022-05-03 ([historical account](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2022/05/03/dockershim-historical-context/), [FAQ](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2022/02/17/dockershim-faq/)). What failed was not container support but the assumption that the core platform should permanently own one vendor-specific adapter. The factor is still present for AI platforms: each provider, framework, or tool can demand a “temporary” exception that becomes a permanent central obligation.

### 2. Kubernetes bundling cloud-provider implementations

Cloud-specific code once shipped in the Kubernetes core release. Different provider and Kubernetes release cadences made this coupling costly, so the project moved provider integrations behind an external cloud-controller-manager interface and assigned implementation ownership to cloud vendors ([Kubernetes administration](https://kubernetes.io/docs/tasks/administer-cluster/running-cloud-controller/), [Kubernetes externalization](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2023/12/14/cloud-provider-integration-changes/)). This was a deliberate federated correction: centralize the contract and interoperability expectations, decentralize provider-specific code and releases. The remaining risk is governance fragmentation—an interface does not itself guarantee equivalent support, conformance, security, or lifecycle behavior across implementations.

### 3. GOV.UK PaaS outliving its original comparative advantage

GOV.UK PaaS was not an operational collapse: GDS reported strong uptime, one major incident, and substantial public-service use. The boundary nonetheless stopped paying because cloud providers lowered entry barriers, departments gained cloud skills, usage did not continue growing as hoped, and the platform required major architectural investment. On 2022-07-12 GDS chose decommissioning over reinvestment ([GDS](https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2022/07/12/why-weve-decided-to-decommission-gov-uk-paas-platform-as-a-service/)). The lesson is stronger than “platforms fail”: a central runtime can succeed technically and still become the wrong ownership arrangement when its users and suppliers change.

### 4. Slack’s first platform iterations becoming legacy obligations

Slack’s 2023-03-21 account describes a broad cloud-engineering remit with overlapping systems, duplicated feature work, unclear support expectations, and difficulty retiring old responsibilities. BuiltBySlack proved demand, but Slack later replaced and retired it; Bedrock Classic was itself put on a deprecation path after Bedrock YAML reached feature parity ([Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/technology-lifecycle/)). The failed assumption was that a useful internal abstraction could simply remain supported indefinitely. Slack’s corrective mechanism was organizational as much as technical: explicit stages, customer expectations, support budgets, feature-complete alternatives, and a retirement decision.

### 5. Backstage’s original backend abstraction requiring a redesign

Backstage’s open-source backend began as flexible patterns and utilities because Spotify’s internal frameworks could not be exported. As adopter count, community, code, and capabilities grew, maintainers said the organically evolved system needed reevaluation and introduced a new backend architecture on 2023-02-15 ([Backstage](https://backstage.io/blog/2023/02/15/backend-system-alpha/)). This is not evidence that Backstage failed. It is evidence that extreme extensibility is also an ownership choice: without sufficiently strong contracts, every plugin author and adopter pays integration complexity; with overly strong contracts, local use cases are blocked.

Across the cases, three factors recur: bundled special cases accrete maintenance, successful abstractions need planned replacement, and migration work is part of platform cost. Spotify’s migration practice reinforces the last point: fragmented deprecation messages and uncoordinated deadlines overwhelmed product teams, so the platform organization created a shared migration map, estimated impact, assigned owners, and tested replacements before pushing adoption ([Spotify migrations](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2020/6/tech-migrations-the-spotify-way)).

The named organizations that recur across more than one precedent are Google (ML debt, TFX, DORA, and platform-engineering guidance), Spotify (Golden Paths, Backstage, migrations, and agent interfaces), CNCF/Kubernetes (platform composition and interface externalization), Uber (successive Michelangelo generations), and Slack (successive internal compute platforms). Their published material is first-party evidence about mechanisms and stated outcomes, not neutral proof that the same design will work elsewhere.

## What's actually new this time

### Genuinely new or materially intensified

- **The user of the platform can now be an agent.** Spotify’s current case exposes catalogue and developer-platform capabilities through MCP and command-line tools so an agent can discover ownership and documentation. That makes machine-readable contracts, deterministic feedback, provenance, and least-privilege execution part of developer experience rather than merely API hygiene ([Spotify Engineering](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)).
- **A tool description can lead to a business action chosen by a probabilistic system.** MCP explicitly separates host responsibilities from focused servers and warns through its authorization model that protected resources need proper token and audience handling. Discovery, transport authorization, user consent, downstream business authorization, and safe execution are therefore distinct layers ([MCP architecture](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/architecture/index), [MCP authorization](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/basic/authorization)).
- **Evaluation is both domain-specific and continuously needed.** Traditional deployment contracts can validate build artefacts and configuration; agent behavior depends on prompts, models, retrieved data, tools, and multi-step trajectories. OpenTelemetry’s active work on agent and GenAI semantic conventions shows that even the shared vocabulary for tracing models and tool calls is still evolving ([OpenTelemetry](https://opentelemetry.io/blog/2025/ai-agent-observability/)).
- **Model suppliers and agent frameworks change faster than most internal platforms.** The MLOps literature already contains many architecture components and variants; agents add host, client, server, tool, prompt, resource, model-router, memory, sandbox, and approval concerns. A broad central abstraction can therefore harden assumptions while the underlying ecosystem is still moving ([MLOps mapping study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19847), [MCP](https://www.anthropic.com/news/model-context-protocol)).

### Old pattern, new branding

- Model access is another scarce or governed infrastructure service; the EC2 precedent still applies: expose a stable service contract, meter it, and avoid owning customer logic merely because the service is centralized ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2006/08/24/announcing-amazon-elastic-compute-cloud-amazon-ec2---beta/)).
- Agent catalogues repeat service-catalogue questions about declared versus observed facts, authoritative sources, staleness, ownership, and deletion. Backstage already documents ingestion errors, orphaning, and the need to keep real-time truth in the systems that produce it ([Backstage entity lifecycle](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/life-of-an-entity/)).
- AI gateways repeat API-gateway and service-mesh questions about routing, policy, telemetry, failure domains, and vendor coupling. A gateway is a runtime dependency, not simply metadata or a portal.
- “One AI platform” repeats the end-to-end MLOps ambition. Uber demonstrates that such integration can work at substantial scale, but its later redesign toward plug-and-play components also demonstrates that an integrated experience need not mean one internally built implementation for every layer ([Uber Michelangelo 2.0](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/from-predictive-to-generative-ai/)).
- Paved roads versus mandates is not an AI-specific debate. Spotify, Team Topologies, Thoughtworks, Google, and DORA all point toward an attractive common path, explicit exceptions, and outcome measurement; controls that prevent high-consequence harm may still be mandatory at their actual enforcement boundary ([Spotify](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2020/08/how-we-use-golden-paths-to-solve-fragmentation-in-our-software-ecosystem), [Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/application-modernization/platform-engineering-control-mechanisms), [DORA](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/)).

## What an experienced observer would expect next

1. **The first contract will attract adjacent consumers and fields.** Backstage began as a catalogue and grew into a hub because ownership metadata became a useful join point for templates, documentation, CI/CD, quality, and migrations ([Spotify](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2021/03/happy-birthday-backstage-spotifys-biggest-open-source-project-grows-up-fast)). An agent descriptor will likely follow the same path. The modal risk is that advisory metadata quietly becomes required for execution before authority, freshness, and failure behavior are designed.

2. **The platform will split “own” into several verbs.** Mature designs distinguish define, discover, provision, operate, authorize, observe, support, fund, and retire. Kubernetes externalized runtime and cloud-provider implementations while retaining core interfaces; Backstage renders data from external sources; MCP separates host, client, and server responsibilities ([Kubernetes](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2023/12/14/cloud-provider-integration-changes/), [Backstage](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/external-integrations/), [MCP](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/architecture/index)). Chapter 4 will probably become clearer when each candidate capability is decomposed along those verbs rather than assigned wholesale to “platform” or “team.”

3. **Paved roads will expand before mandates do.** The historical route is documentation, templates, adapters, and self-service for common work, with local alternatives remaining possible. Mandatory controls appear where shared risk or irreversible action makes optional behavior unacceptable, and Google’s control taxonomy distinguishes steering paths from hard-stop guardrails, recovery safety nets, and human checkpoints ([Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques/incremental-developer-platform), [Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/application-modernization/platform-engineering-control-mechanisms)).

4. **Broad integrated platforms will persist mainly where scale and lifecycle repetition are exceptional.** Uber’s successive Michelangelo versions and Google’s TFX show the value of shared training, deployment, and monitoring mechanisms at large scale, but Michelangelo’s move toward commodity plug-and-play components suggests that even a broad experience will mix build and buy ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/from-predictive-to-generative-ai/), [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/opml19/presentation/baylor)). Smaller organizations should be expected to extend cloud and developer platforms with a contract, gateway, or evaluation service rather than recreate that breadth.

5. **Existing developer platforms will become agent-facing before a separate portal wins.** Spotify’s 2026-06-03 report is an early concrete precedent: expose catalogue and platform capabilities through MCP and CLI while running the agent in a separate harness ([Spotify Engineering](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)). That preserves one ownership graph and one set of engineering standards for humans and agents instead of duplicating them in an AI-specific catalogue.

6. **Special integrations will be pushed out of the core behind conformance contracts.** Kubernetes’ removal of `dockershim` and in-tree cloud providers is the modal precedent ([Kubernetes dockershim](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2022/05/03/dockershim-historical-context/), [Kubernetes cloud providers](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2023/12/14/cloud-provider-integration-changes/)). For AI, model-provider adapters, MCP servers, evaluation plugins, and telemetry exporters will likely be federated, while the platform retains schemas, compatibility tests, approved distributions, and lifecycle policy.

7. **Decommissioning will become the test of whether the boundary preserves freedom.** GOV.UK PaaS and Slack show that a platform must be able to identify consumers, fund dual running, provide a replacement or supported exit, and stop support on a declared schedule ([GDS](https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2022/07/12/why-weve-decided-to-decommission-gov-uk-paas-platform-as-a-service/), [Slack](https://slack.engineering/technology-lifecycle/)). An AI capability that cannot export its metadata, evidence, policy, and evaluation history will make nominal model choice irrelevant because the organization cannot actually leave.

The historically grounded default is therefore not “centralize the six capabilities” or “leave all six local.” It is: centralize the smallest stable contract or expensive repeated mechanism; keep domain meaning and accountability with the workflow owner; place enforcement at the boundary that can still prevent harm; federate provider-specific implementations where a conformance contract is sufficient; and require every shared capability to justify its ongoing ownership and exit cost.
