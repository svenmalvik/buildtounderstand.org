# Three failures: capacity, ownership, assurance

**Question this document answers:** Is there evidence that the review problem is actually three different problems, and what supports each one?

**Chapter it feeds:** `_explorations/what-is-the-second-pair-of-eyes-for.md`, chapter 02, "Three Failures That Look Like One"

**Last updated:** 2026-09-17

Scope: the evidence base for each of the three failures separately. The three-way split itself is an analytical framework carried over from the capacity research in the `vce-context` worktree, not a finding with prevalence estimates attached. What is new here is that two of the three now have direct measurements.

## Main findings

**The ownership failure has a clean survey pairing from one instrument.** Of 1,149 developers, 96 per cent said they do not fully trust that AI output is correct, while only 48 per cent said they always verify it before committing. Same survey, same respondents. People distrust the code and submit it anyway.

**The assurance failure now has a direct measurement, and a good one.** A longitudinal study of 400 reviewers across 11,429 reviews found approval rates rising while inline comments fell, and it rules out the three obvious alternative explanations.

**The capacity failure is covered by the previous section's research** and is not repeated here.

## The ownership failure

**The survey pairing.** Sonar "State of Code" developer survey, 1,149 respondents, page metadata gives a creation date of 8 January 2026. **SELF-REPORTED.** Sonar's own wording:

> "although 72% of developers who have tried AI use it nearly every day, 96% of developers don't fully trust its output, and yet only 48% always verify it before committing"

The page card states it more plainly: "Less than half of developers always verify AI code before committing it."

**Two limits that must travel with this.** The claim is "always verify", which is not the same as "understand", and the section argues about understanding. The underlying question wording is not public, because the report sits behind a download form.

**A trap on that page.** Its statistics render through JavaScript counters. A summarising fetch of the page returned 68, 60 and 58 per cent for the three headline figures, which are wrong. The correct labels from the raw markup are: 96 per cent "don't fully trust that AI-generated code is functionally correct", 61 per cent "agree that AI often produces code that looks correct but isn't reliable", and 57 per cent worry about exposing sensitive data. Do not cite 68 or 60 for this survey.

**Corroborating trust figures, different instruments.** Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025, question wording "How much do you trust the accuracy of the output from AI tools as part of your development workflow?", n = 33,244: highly trust 3.1 per cent, somewhat trust 29.6 per cent, somewhat distrust 26.1 per cent, highly distrust 19.6 per cent. Those four sum to 78.4 per cent, so an unlabelled middle category exists and no "neither" figure should be reported. Its top frustration, n = 31,476, is "AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite" at 66 per cent. The DevOps Research and Assessment 2025 survey of nearly 5,000 professionals reports that "30% report little or no trust in the code generated by AI".

**What nobody has measured.** No human-subjects study was found measuring whether an author can explain code an AI wrote for them. The nearest item measures the code rather than the person: a March 2026 study of 591 pull requests and 5,027 Python files found "over 90% of constructs falling into the A1 and A2 categories, and less than 1% classified as Mastery (C2)". Its framing is useful for the chapter, describing a shift in "software developers' roles from code authors to code reviewers".

## The assurance failure

**The direct measurement.** Yu, Liu, Jiang, Jia, Wang, Qian and Chen, "Habituation at the Gate: Rising Approval and Declining Scrutiny in Human Review of AI Agent Code", arXiv 2606.22721, submitted 21 June 2026, accepted at the KDD 2026 Workshop on Agentic Software Engineering. Verified directly against the abstract on 17 September 2026. Sample: 400 repeat reviewers, 11,429 reviews, seven months, within-reviewer design.

> "we observe a population-level shift in approval rate from 30.1% to 36.8% (Wilcoxon signed-rank p < 10^{-6} on paired shifts)"

> "Pooled by within-reviewer experience decile, the cumulative gap reaches +14.5 pp from first to tenth decile."

> "review latency increases rather than decreases (+3.5x), while inline comment volume decreases (-22%, p=0.0014), suggesting reviewers spend more time in queue but less time actively inspecting code"

> "most consistent with reflexive habituation under growing workload rather than rational trust calibration alone"

**Why this is stronger than a correlation.** The abstract names three controls: "This shift is experience-driven (persists after controlling for calendar time), agent-specific (human PR approval rates decline over the same period), and not explained by PR difficulty (median PR size is flat)." The agent-specific control is the most useful one, because approval of human-written pull requests moved the opposite way in the same window.

**A numeric collision to avoid.** This paper's early-period approval rate of 30.1 per cent is numerically identical to the 30.1 per cent figure in the May 2026 paper on recorded review activity, which measures something entirely different. Never let both appear near each other without distinguishing them.

**A companion paper is the same measurement, not a second one.** A 5 September 2026 paper by overlapping authors uses the same 11,429 reviews and reports approval rising "from 30.5% in reviewers' early periods to 36.6% in late periods", adding that "language shifts follow rather than precede changes in approval behavior". Treat the two as one result.

**Counter-evidence worth carrying.** An eye-tracking study, "Same Scrutiny, More Time", 25 June 2026, found that when code was labelled as machine-written, "the thoroughness of code review did not change for participants, they spent more time fixating on LLM-labelled code", and it "reveals a gap between reviewers' intentions and actual reviewing behaviour". Labelling changed attention allocation without changing measured thoroughness. Participant count not stated in the abstract.

**Agreement with automated recommendations is high.** A 27 July 2026 within-subjects study of 34 participants found that "moderate explanations (B) achieve the highest agreement (89.22%)", while noting that "more explanation prompts developers to question AI recommendations more frequently".

**Attention limits independent of AI.** A 2022 study of 219,476 pull requests across 138 Java projects, plus a controlled experiment with 106 participants, found reviewers "have 64% lower odds to identify it when its file is last as opposed to first". Useful because it shows review depth was already positional before any of this.

**Borrowed from another domain, labelled as such.** Parasuraman and Manzey, Human Factors, 2010: "Automation bias results in making both omission and commission errors when decision aids are imperfect", and it "cannot be prevented by training or instructions, and can affect decision making in individuals as well as in teams". Goddard, Roudsari and Wyatt, JAMIA, January 2012, a systematic review of 74 papers from 13,821 retrieved, defines automation bias as "the tendency to over-rely on automation". Use only if the code-review evidence is presented first.

## The author's own mistake

Confirmed by Sven on 17 September 2026 and placed with the assurance failure: he approved a change because the tests were green, without reading it. The approval was recorded; what it recorded was confidence in the tests rather than a reading of the change.

The two other candidates considered are **not** his experience and must never be written: opening a pull request from agent output he could not explain, and introducing a review rule that people bypassed.

## Why the split is the point

Each failure responds to a different remedy, which is the section's argument:

| Failure | What it is | What fixes it | What does not |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Capacity | More changes than qualified reviewers can examine | More reviewer time, less incoming work, or less required depth | Teaching authors; adding an approval rule |
| Ownership | The author does not understand what they submit | Author responsibility, self-review, disclosure | More reviewers, who absorb the cost instead |
| Assurance | An approval is recorded where no examination happened | Measuring review depth, not counting approvals | Another required approval, which adds a record |

The framework is analytical. It is not a claim about how common each failure is, and no source found estimates their relative prevalence.

## Confidence and gaps

| Claim | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| 96 per cent do not fully trust AI output, 48 per cent always verify | High for the survey. Self-reported, one instrument, question wording not public. |
| Developers submit code they do not understand | Inferred. The survey measures verification, not understanding. State it as verification. |
| Reviewers approve agent code more readily over time | High. Within-reviewer design, 400 reviewers, three controls, workshop-accepted. |
| Reviewers inspect agent code less over time | High for inline comments, which fell 22 per cent. Comment volume is a proxy for attention. |
| Habituation is proven to cause escaped defects | No. The study measures approval and comment behaviour, not outcomes. |
| Labelling code as AI-written reduces scrutiny | Contradicted. The eye-tracking study found thoroughness unchanged and attention increased. |
| Automation bias applies to code review | Partly. Direct code-review evidence now exists; the classic literature is from other domains. |
| The three failures occur at known rates | Not established. No source estimates prevalence. |
