# Stakeholders, money, and power

Research cutoff: 2026-07-31. Scope: a generic enterprise AI platform, not a named company or procurement. Consequently, enterprise-specific headcount, prices, contracts, data locations, and decision owners are **Unknown/Undisclosed**. The useful result is a decision and total-cost map showing what must be made explicit before the chapter's claim of “freedom” can be tested.

## Bottom line

The platform will preserve freedom only if the right to leave is funded, technically rehearsed, and governed by people other than the platform's builders. A versioned contract can reduce integration work, but it also gives its maintainers agenda-setting power: they decide what is expressible, when a change is breaking, and whether a non-standard workflow receives support. This is not hypothetical platform anxiety. DORA's 2024 research associated internal-platform use with higher individual, team, and organizational performance while also associating it with lower throughput and change stability; developer independence recovered some of the benefit ([DORA 2024 report](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/2024-dora-accelerate-state-of-devops-report.pdf)).

The recurring costs are broader than inference: platform product and engineering labor, consumer integration, security/privacy review, cost attribution, on-call work, exception handling, duplicated operation during migration, and deletion verification. Public surveys make the omitted labor visible: 69% of developers in an Atlassian/DX/Wakefield survey said they lost at least eight hours a week to inefficiencies, while a New Relic/ETR survey found engineering teams spending 30% of their time on disruptions ([Atlassian, 2025-01-14](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/development/developer-experience-report-2024); [New Relic, 2024-10-22](https://newrelic.com/press-release/20241022)). These are contextual, vendor-sponsored surveys rather than forecasts for this platform, but they show why labor shifted to consumers and operators must be counted.

Legal obligations also follow roles and uses, not the internal label “platform.” Under the EU AI Act, an organization can be a provider, deployer, importer, or distributor depending on what it changes and supplies; providers and deployers of high-risk systems have distinct duties, and substantial modification can shift provider responsibility ([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex%3A32024R1689)). Under GDPR, a controller remains responsible for choosing processors and transfer arrangements; an internal gateway does not transfer that accountability to its operator ([GDPR Articles 28 and 44](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN-DE/TXT/?from=EN&uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679); [EDPB Opinion 22/2024](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/edpb-adopts-opinion-processors-guidelines-legitimate-interest-statement-draft_ga)).

**Asymmetries.** Providers know their infrastructure, feature-retention paths, and planned deprecations better than buyers; central platform teams know the shared layer better than product teams; product teams know workflow context and affected users better than central teams; regulators can impose duties that neither engineering group may waive. Large providers also negotiate from a stronger position than smaller customers: the US Treasury specifically found outsized provider bargaining power, especially against smaller financial institutions, and the UK CMA found significant market power for the two largest cloud providers ([US Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy1252); [CMA](https://www.gov.uk/cma-cases/cloud-services-market-investigation)). A fair governance model must therefore give consumer teams representation in contract changes, give risk owners bounded authority over control outcomes, and give the organization an independently funded exit path.

### Minimal TCO equation

For a period *t*, measure:

`net leverage = avoided duplicated work + reusable control value + delivery value - (platform build + platform run + consumer integration + verification + support/incidents + governance + exception + migration reserve + exit + harm remediation)`

The platform preserves freedom only if the equation is positive **and** exit performance remains inside a pre-agreed budget and time threshold. Savings cannot be claimed merely because work moved from product teams into an unmeasured central queue.

## Power and authority map

The following is a **recommended allocation**, not a description of an existing organization. It separates outcome authority from implementation authority so that “mandatory safety” does not become a blank cheque for architecture centralization.

| Decision | Proposes / maintains | Must approve | Must be consulted | Required evidence and freedom guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Contract/schema changes** | Platform product owner with a public RFC and reference implementation | Contract governance group with elected consumer-team representation; relevant control owner only for fields enforcing its outcome | Application teams, SRE, data governance, FinOps, adapter maintainers | Compatibility tests against at least two implementations; migration guide; deprecated-field telemetry; support window; cost estimate; an ADR recording rejected alternatives. Three veto boundaries follow. A platform team may reject an implementation that violates an approved interface, but it should not unilaterally define business risk. A control owner may require a safety/privacy outcome, but it should not dictate a single vendor or framework unless alternatives cannot satisfy the outcome. A business sponsor may accept delivery trade-offs, but it cannot waive statutory duties or expose another team to unfunded operational risk.

## Under-recognised winners and losers

### Winners

- **Small teams with serious control requirements** may be the largest winners: reusable identity, audit, privacy, and incident capabilities let them attempt workflows they could not responsibly operate alone. This is leverage only if onboarding and exception queues do not consume the saved work.
- **FinOps and enterprise architecture** gain earlier influence over provider and placement decisions. In the 2026 FinOps survey, 78% of practices reported into the CTO/CIO organization, and respondents with executive engagement reported materially more influence over provider selection ([State of FinOps 2026](https://data.finops.org/)). This can improve total-cost decisions, but it also moves power toward central technology leadership.
- **Existing cloud/model vendors** can win even from a nominally vendor-neutral contract if the first adapter is best supported, proprietary features leak through, or committed spend makes alternatives uneconomic. The CMA and US Treasury findings show why commercial portability must be assessed separately from API portability ([CMA](https://www.gov.uk/cma-cases/cloud-services-market-investigation); [US Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy1252)).
- **Audit, security, and legal functions** gain reusable evidence and a clearer inventory. Their benefit should be measured as avoided repeated review, not assumed from centralization.

### Losers

- **Teams at the long tail of requirements** lose when the shared contract expresses only the median workflow and exception lead time exceeds direct implementation time.
- **Platform on-call engineers** inherit the combined failure surface of CI validation, gateways, policy engines, telemetry, adapters, and upstream providers. This burden is invisible if the business case counts only reduced application-team toil.
- **Non-adopters and teams that bypass the platform** can lose support, budget, or audit legitimacy even when adoption is formally optional. “Optional” therefore requires a documented supported-alternative policy, not only the absence of a technical block.
- **Affected people** can lose through scaled, consistent error. Standard controls may make deployment faster without making the underlying decision fair, accurate, or contestable; the AI Act assigns deployers context-specific duties because providers cannot see the full use context ([AI Act recitals 91–93 and Article 26](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex%3A32024R1689)).
- **Smaller enterprise buyers** can face weaker contract leverage and fewer staff to maintain multi-provider options. US Treasury explicitly found both skills gaps and outsized CSP bargaining power, especially for smaller financial institutions ([Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy1252)).
- **Framework and specialist-tool maintainers** may be displaced by the blessed adapter while still being asked to support edge cases that the platform cannot express.

No party was contacted, and none is described as declining to comment. The items below are therefore **evidence gaps and under-represented roles**, not named-party silence.

## What Chapter 5 can responsibly conclude from this angle

1. **Freedom is an operating property, not an API property.** Require measured ticket-free completion, exception latency, provider-switch rehearsal, and deletion evidence. DORA's mixed platform findings and independence result support measuring both productivity and delivery/stability ([DORA](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/2024-dora-accelerate-state-of-devops-report.pdf)).
2. **The smallest governance layer still needs explicit owners.** Name contract, risk, budget, incident, data, exception, and retirement owners; no one owner should control all seven.
3. **Portable syntax is necessary but insufficient.** Commercial commitments, identity, telemetry semantics, safety behavior, state, evaluation baselines, data paths, and provider-only features can preserve lock-in after an API is abstracted. The CMA, Treasury, and provider documents supply concrete reasons to test those layers ([CMA](https://www.gov.uk/cma-cases/cloud-services-market-investigation); [Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy1252); [Google](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/vertex-ai-zero-data-retention); [Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/responsible-ai/openai/data-privacy); [OpenAI](https://platform.openai.com/docs/models/default-usage-policies-by-endpoint)).
4. **Mandatory controls need a named legal or risk basis and an appeal path.** Otherwise preferences can masquerade as obligations. The AI Act and GDPR assign duties by role and use; NIST makes governance continuous and organization-wide ([AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex%3A32024R1689); [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN-DE/TXT/?from=EN&uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679); [NIST](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-1.pdf)).
5. **Exit must be a prepaid product feature.** Hold a migration reserve, maintain adapters and exports, rehearse annually, and publish actual elapsed time, labor, feature loss, and recovery result. Statutory switching rights and reduced egress charges do not perform the customer's migration ([EU Data Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/2854/oj?locale=en)).
6. **Count affected people in “engineering freedom.”** A team is not free to externalize verification, discrimination, privacy, or redress costs onto workers and customers. The platform should preserve team choice inside non-waivable rights and safety boundaries, with affected-person evidence in the decision record ([AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex%3A32024R1689)).
