# Stakeholders, money, and power: the smallest useful AI platform

This is an evergreen design question rather than a transaction with one buyer, operator, and contract. The useful stakeholder unit is therefore the **operating model**: who performs recurring work, who can compel others to use the result, who carries failure risk, and who can stop paying. The four models compared below are:

1. **Contracts-only:** shared metadata, schemas, policies, templates, and conformance tests, consumed through an existing portal or source-control workflow.
2. **Narrow managed capability:** a small service such as an authenticated model gateway with usage attribution, policy, and basic telemetry.
3. **Broad integrated platform:** catalogue, portal, gateway, agent runtime, evaluation, observability, governance, and deployment workflows under one product boundary.
4. **No dedicated AI platform:** teams consume model and cloud services directly and reuse ordinary identity, delivery, observability, security, and FinOps systems.

The central money finding is not that one model is always cheaper. It is that each model places the same classes of work—schema stewardship, integration, evaluation, access control, cost allocation, audit evidence, incident response, upgrades, and exit—on different budgets. “Smallest” is meaningful only when those transferred costs are counted.

## Geography and jurisdiction

**Optional per research plan; produced because jurisdiction can impose a capability floor.** There is no single subject company, so these are decision layers rather than claims that every organization must implement the same controls.

| Layer | Location(s) | Jurisdiction(s) | Source | Why it matters to “smallest” |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contract-only metadata and policy | Usually the organization’s existing source-control, identity, and portal estate | The organization’s existing corporate, employment, data-protection, and sectoral jurisdictions | Backstage makes source-controlled YAML the source of truth and assigns maintenance to component-owning teams ([Backstage catalogue](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/)). | Reusing an existing governed system can avoid a new data plane; it does not avoid ownership, validation, retention, or access-control work. |
| Model API and narrow gateway | Gateway region plus every model-provider endpoint it can route to | Customer jurisdiction, gateway-provider terms, model-provider terms, and any cross-border transfer rules | Bedrock exposes region- and model-specific offerings and pricing, including cross-region inference ([AWS pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/bedrock/pricing/)). | A “single endpoint” can conceal multiple processing locations and subprocessors; routing policy and data-flow inventory may be the minimum, not the UI. |
| Broad integrated platform | Vendor control plane, customer-selected data plane, replicas, backups, telemetry, and support locations | Operator incorporation, hosting region, customer jurisdiction, and support-personnel jurisdictions | Google’s filing describes Google Cloud as consumption and subscriptions spanning infrastructure, Vertex AI, Gemini Enterprise, cybersecurity, data, and Workspace rather than one AI-only service ([Alphabet 2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1652044/000165204426000018/goog-20251231.htm)). | “Region” is not a complete jurisdictional answer. Buyers need the contract, subprocessor list, support-access model, and service-specific data terms. |
| US-provider compelled-access reach | Provider-controlled data wherever stored | United States plus any applicable executive-agreement partner | The US Department of Justice describes covered providers as public communications or remote-computing providers that process or store data ([US DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-oia/regarding-cloud-act-executive-agreements)). | EU-region storage alone does not answer whether a US-headquartered provider can be compelled to produce data it controls. |
| EU deployer and users | European Economic Area, or systems placed on or used in the EU market | EU AI Act, GDPR, national labor/sectoral law, and national supervisory authorities | The Commission says the AI framework can reach public and private actors inside and outside the EU that place systems/models on the EU market or use systems in the EU ([European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/navigating-ai-act)). | The minimum may need model/use-case inventory, role assignment, logs, instructions, or human-oversight evidence even when no internal runtime is built. |
| AI Act model-versus-system supervision | EU market | EU AI Office for general-purpose model obligations; national authorities for much system-level enforcement | General-purpose-model obligations applied from **2025-08-02**, with full Commission enforcement from **2026-08-02** ([European Commission guidance](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/guidelines-obligations-general-purpose-ai-providers)). | Buying a model does not transfer every deployer duty to the provider; building a broad platform does not automatically make its central team the legal risk owner. |
| Contract governing law and liability | Contract-specific | Contract-specific; often provider-selected unless negotiated | **Unknown:** no contract, RFP, DPA, or order form is attached to this evergreen chapter. | SLA credits, audit rights, termination assistance, data return/deletion, indemnities, and usage commitments cannot be inferred from product pages. |
| Sanctions, export control, and dual use | Workload-, model-, chip-, user-, and destination-specific | Customer and supplier export-control/sanctions regimes | **n/a at this level:** no model export, restricted destination, chip purchase, or designated counterparty is proposed. | Treat this as a use-case gate, not a reason to impose an export-control subsystem on every internal experiment. |

The important asymmetry is between **where data is stored** and **which entities or people can access or be compelled to disclose it**. The second asymmetry is between the model provider’s obligations and the deploying organization’s obligations: a managed platform can supply evidence and controls, but it cannot accept business-use risk on behalf of an employer, bank, health provider, or public authority merely by contract ([European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/navigating-ai-act)).

### What each model makes visible—and hides

| Operating model | Central budget sees | Work likely pushed elsewhere | Quiet financial risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contracts-only** | Schema owner, conformance tooling, catalogue integration | Component teams maintain metadata; security and audit teams chase noncompliance; application teams integrate runtime controls | “Free” YAML becomes stale or performative; every consumer interprets ambiguous semantics differently; migrations are paid from many team budgets |
| **Narrow managed** | Gateway runtime, identity, provider routing, budgets, usage telemetry, on-call | Application teams retain prompt/tool design, evaluation, end-user UX, data quality, workflow operations, and business-risk acceptance | The narrow service becomes critical infrastructure; its owner inherits incidents and model/provider churn without owning downstream quality |
| **Broad integrated** | Product team, vendor contract, platform SRE, migrations, support, governance, portal and runtime roadmap | Domain teams still supply business context, evaluation criteria, metadata, and exception cases | Common-mode failure, scope growth, unused features, vendor lock-in, and multi-year migration; apparent local savings can be offset by a permanent central product |
| **No dedicated platform** | Provider invoices within team/cloud budgets | Every application team repeats procurement, access, telemetry, evaluation, audit, FinOps, incident, and exit work; central functions reconcile after the fact | Spend and data flows are discovered late; high performers may cope while novice or non-engineer builders receive no usable path |

## Power and authority map

### Decision 1: whether there is a dedicated AI platform at all

- **Formal decider:** accountable engineering/technology executive and budget owner—typically CTO, CIO, VP Engineering, or a delegated architecture/investment council. A platform is a continuing product expense, so approval should be operating funding, not only project capital; Puppet explicitly notes that platform engineering often requires moving away from time-limited project funding ([Puppet report](https://www.puppet.com/system/files/report-puppet-sodor-2023-platform-engineering.pdf)).
- **Approvals, in sequence:** documented repeated constraint → baseline and affected users → comparison with existing-platform extension and no-platform alternatives → security/privacy/legal and finance assessment → named product and operational owner → funding and success/exit criteria.
- **Veto holders:** budget owner; accountable security/privacy or sectoral risk owner for prohibited data/use; enterprise architecture when the proposal duplicates an existing platform; procurement for an unacceptable contract.
- **Soft influence without formal authority:** AI enthusiasts, consultants, analysts, platform vendors, and a newly hired platform leader. In Puppet’s survey, **10%** cited a consulting recommendation and **8%** a newly hired senior advocate as triggers—evidence of influence, not evidence that the resulting platform was necessary ([Puppet report](https://www.puppet.com/system/files/report-puppet-sodor-2023-platform-engineering.pdf)).
- **Reversal conditions:** the measured constraint disappears; adoption is mandated but retention/task success remains poor; total distributed-plus-central cost exceeds the baseline; or an existing developer/security/FinOps platform can absorb the proven capability.

### Decision 2: what enters the shared contract or platform scope

- **Formal decider:** platform product owner for product scope, with the relevant security, data, finance, and architecture owners for their policies. A schema field without a named consumer and decision is inventory theater.
- **Approvals, in sequence:** user research → smallest workflow hypothesis → technical and policy design → two or more real adopter teams → measurable task-success and independence test → general availability. DORA recommends starting with one common golden path and says extensibility prevents the central team becoming the bottleneck ([DORA](https://dora.dev/capabilities/platform-engineering/)).
- **Veto holders:** application/domain team for the accuracy of its metadata and evaluation criteria; security/privacy for access and sensitive-data policy; runtime owner for operational feasibility.
- **Soft influence without formal authority:** commercial vendors have a reason to define maturity as adoption of more platform categories. Humanitec’s own model links maturity to tool adoption, and the peer-reviewed review flags that vendor bias explicitly ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2026.1814498/full)).
- **Reversal conditions:** low voluntary use, rising exceptions, reduced developer independence, declining delivery stability, or a field that nobody uses to make a decision. Backstage demonstrates that a contract still needs validation, ingestion, orphan handling, ownership, and schema evolution ([Backstage configuration](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/configuration/)).

### Decision 3: whether use is mandatory and who grants exceptions

- **Formal decider:** the accountable policy/risk owner can mandate a control; the platform product owner should not create policy merely because the platform can enforce it.
- **Approvals, in sequence:** identify the risk and governing obligation → define the minimum control and evidence → document the normal path → document exception authority and expiry → test developer task success → audit both compliance and bypass.
- **Veto holders:** statutory/sectoral risk owner for genuinely prohibited uses; business owner for accepting residual business risk; worker representatives where workplace monitoring or automated employment decisions invoke local rights.
- **Soft influence without formal authority:** platform teams can shape defaults and visibility; application teams can create de facto veto power by bypassing an unusable path. Spotify’s experience was that Backstage had to be the better option, not the only option ([Spotify Engineering](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2021/5/a-product-story-the-lessons-of-backstage-and-spotifys-autonomous-culture)).
- **Reversal conditions:** a mandate lowers satisfaction without reducing the named risk, bypass grows, or exception volume shows the “golden path” is a golden cage. The systematic review reports that mandated platforms have worse outcomes than value-led adoption and that high adoption can simply reflect compulsion ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2026.1814498/full)).

### Decision 4: shrink, replace, or remove a capability

- **Formal decider:** the service/product owner proposes; the accountable technology and business-risk owners approve; dependent product owners accept migration or residual risk.
- **Approvals, in sequence:** dependency inventory → usage and decision-consumer evidence → incident/audit/financial impact → replacement or no-replacement plan → contract and data-exit review → migration → decommission verification.
- **Veto holders:** a downstream service owner with an unresolved critical dependency; legal/records owner where evidence must be retained; procurement where commitments or termination fees remain.
- **Soft influence without formal authority:** sunk-cost owners, vendor customer-success teams, and teams that enjoy the central budget while externalizing metadata or exception work.
- **Reversal conditions:** failed migration, unowned control gap, or total exit cost exceeding the remaining avoided cost. The Frontiers review warns that deep integrations produce lock-in while shallow integrations may fail to provide enough value—an explicit trade-off rather than a maturity ladder ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2026.1814498/full)).

## Under-recognised winners and losers

The quiet winner in a contracts-only design is the **existing platform estate**—source control, identity, Backstage, observability, and FinOps—which gains relevance without a new product boundary, while its maintainers quietly inherit schema, ingestion, migration, and support work. The quiet winner in a broad design is the vendor whose AI entry point expands consumption of cloud, data, security, and support products; broad cloud revenue must not be mistaken for AI-platform revenue. The quiet losers are application teams asked to keep metadata and evaluation evidence current without capacity, and platform teams asked to run an internal product “part time.” Finance, audit, and incident response are exposed without consent when direct team adoption produces spend and data flows they can see only after the fact. End users are the most consequential hidden stakeholder: they neither choose the internal platform nor benefit from its adoption metric, but they bear the outcome when centralized defaults or decentralized improvisation fail.
