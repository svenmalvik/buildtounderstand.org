# History and precedent: what does “smallest” mean?

## Long arc

The platform story did not begin with AI. Its modern form grew out of attempts to remove the repeated hand-offs and bespoke operating work that separated application developers from production. The first DevOpsDays, held on `2009-10-30` and `2009-10-31`, explicitly brought development and operations practitioners together; its programme already featured reusable behavioural checks, automated infrastructure, continuous-delivery pipelines, and a standardized self-service request system ([Patrick Debois’s announcement](https://groups.google.com/g/agile-system-administration/c/hp7WBg4uCJI), [conference programme](https://legacy.devopsdays.org/events/2009-ghent/program)). This matters because the original target was organizational friction and repeated work, not a portal or a product category.

The early cloud platforms then encoded a “paved path” as an application contract. Heroku’s Twelve-Factor methodology required applications to declare dependencies, separate configuration from code, expose a process model, and tolerate ephemeral runtime conditions; buildpacks translated those conventions into runnable artifacts ([Heroku’s retrospective](https://www.heroku.com/blog/twelve-factor-apps/), [Cloud Foundry’s buildpack history](https://docs.cloudfoundry.org/buildpacks/index.html)). The important precedent is not that everyone needs a PaaS. It is that a compact contract can make many implementations portable and automatable, while a runtime may remain replaceable behind that contract.

Netflix made the social contract more explicit. Its “paved road” was a set of centrally supported tools that teams were free to leave if they accepted the maintenance burden; Netflix said adoption depended on the road being compelling and reducing cognitive load, not on a mandate ([Netflix, “How We Build Code”](https://netflixtechblog.com/how-we-build-code-at-netflix-c5d9bd727f15)). Its later account of full-cycle development drew the boundary carefully: central teams build reusable capabilities only when needs recur across multiple product teams, while specific needs stay local; Netflix also warned that full-cycle ownership can produce burnout when tooling and operating practices do not absorb enough work ([Netflix, “Full Cycle Developers”](https://netflixtechblog.com/full-cycle-developers-at-netflix-a08c31f83249)). Google’s SRE practice contributed another contract-shaped precedent: SLOs and error budgets define the behaviour users need and trigger action without prescribing one user interface or one implementation ([Google SRE, “Service Level Objectives”](https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/)).

Platform engineering eventually named the minimal version directly. Team Topologies defines a “thinnest viable platform” as the smallest set of APIs, documentation, and tools that accelerates stream-aligned teams, and says it can be as small as a wiki page; the platform should be “just thick enough,” self-service, and managed as a product ([Team Topologies mini-book](https://teamtopologies.com/s/Organization-Dynamics-with-Team-Topologies-Mini-book-MB80.pdf)). This is practitioner guidance rather than a controlled study. The best broad quantitative evidence remains mixed: DORA reports associations between internal-platform use and improved individual, team, and organizational performance, but also lower change stability and throughput unless the platform preserves developer independence ([DORA research summary](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/)). Puppet’s survey likewise says merely having a platform team does not unlock high performance; self-service platform use distinguished stronger and weaker mid-level organizations, but the report is observational and vendor-sponsored ([Puppet report](https://www.puppet.com/sites/default/files/pdfs/report-puppet-sodor-2023-platform-engineering.pdf)).

Machine-learning platforms added real technical needs, but also repeated the same centralization cycle. Google’s research catalogued ML-specific debt—data dependencies, feedback loops, undeclared consumers, configuration, and changing external conditions ([Sculley et al.](https://research.google/pubs/hidden-technical-debt-in-machine-learning-systems/)). Uber’s Michelangelo and Google’s TFX responded with end-to-end platforms; TFX reported reducing time to production from months to weeks in one Google Play case, alongside reduced custom code and a reported `2%` increase in installs, but this was a platform-team case study rather than an independent controlled comparison ([Uber Michelangelo](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/michelangelo-machine-learning-platform/), [TFX paper page](https://research.google.com/pubs/pub46484.html)). Netflix then supplied a thinner counter-precedent: Metaflow was deliberately a Python library over existing infrastructure, preserved arbitrary Python business logic, integrated with notebooks instead of shipping a one-size-fits-all UI, and left the underlying cloud and scheduler replaceable ([Netflix Metaflow account](https://netflixtechblog.com/open-sourcing-metaflow-a-human-centric-framework-for-data-science-fa72e04a5d9)).

The latest agent wave begins with another contract, not necessarily another platform. Anthropic released MCP on `2024-11-25` as an open protocol for connecting AI applications to tools and data, explicitly aiming to replace bespoke connectors ([Anthropic announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/model-context-protocol)). That resembles API, metadata, and build contracts from earlier platform history. What differs is that the consumer choosing and invoking the tool can be a probabilistic model: current MCP guidance therefore has to specify delegated authorization, token audience binding, per-call mediation, and human confirmation in ways a passive catalogue descriptor does not ([MCP authorization specification](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/draft/basic/authorization), [MCP client security guidance](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/docs/develop/clients/client-best-practices)).

## Direct precedents

| Date | Precedent and cast | What happened | How it ended / current state | What changed after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `2009-10-30` | DevOpsDays Ghent; Patrick Debois; development and operations practitioners | A community convened around collapsing the dev/ops hand-off and reusing automation, behavioural checks, deployment pipelines, and self-service infrastructure interfaces. | The event became a continuing international series rather than a deployed platform ([DevOpsDays history](https://devopsdays.org/about)). | It established the older problem beneath platform engineering: shared capabilities should remove coordination and repeat work across delivery and operations. |
| `2013-08-15` | Heroku; Adam Wiggins; application developers | Heroku documented how Twelve-Factor conventions and buildpacks formed a narrow contract between applications and a managed runtime. | The methodology outlived its original Heroku context; buildpacks were adopted by Cloud Foundry and other platforms ([Heroku](https://www.heroku.com/blog/twelve-factor-apps/), [Cloud Foundry](https://docs.cloudfoundry.org/buildpacks/index.html)). | A contract plus automation proved able to provide a golden path without dictating application business logic; portability became an explicit design goal. |
| `2016-03-09` | Netflix Engineering Tools; service teams; Nebula, Jenkins, Spinnaker | Netflix described an optional paved road: supported, integrated tools that reduced boilerplate and cognitive load, while teams retained freedom to leave it. | It remained an operating model, not a one-time migration. Adoption was earned by value; off-road teams owned the extra maintenance ([Netflix](https://netflixtechblog.com/how-we-build-code-at-netflix-c5d9bd727f15)). | “Platform” became a supported default with an explicit escape hatch rather than a mandatory universal stack. |
| `2017-09-05` | Uber Michelangelo; data scientists; application engineers | Uber replaced desktop-scale experiments and bespoke serving systems with a shared service covering data, training, evaluation, deployment, prediction, and monitoring. | Michelangelo became Uber’s de facto ML system for dozens of teams at the time of publication; the account does not provide an independent counterfactual ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/michelangelo-machine-learning-platform/)). | It demonstrated that repeated, consequential ML production gaps can justify a broad platform—but only at Uber’s scale and after concrete failures were visible. |
| `2018-05-17` | Netflix Edge Engineering; centralized Cloud Platform, Reliability, and Engineering Tools teams | Netflix documented “full-cycle” ownership: product teams operate what they build, while central teams turn recurring needs into reusable building blocks. | It remained a deliberately mixed model. Netflix reported faster feedback but also cognitive-load and burnout risks when operating work was not well contained ([Netflix](https://netflixtechblog.com/full-cycle-developers-at-netflix-a08c31f83249)). | The platform boundary became empirical: centralize needs recurring across teams; leave narrow needs local; continually adapt rather than assume universal coverage. |
| `2019-12-03` | Netflix ML Infrastructure; data scientists; Metaflow; AWS | Netflix open-sourced a “deceptively simple” Python library that hid data, compute, orchestration, and versioning mechanics while preserving ordinary Python and notebook workflows. | Metaflow remains maintained and supports multiple clouds and Kubernetes; the project says it is used by hundreds of companies, a first-party adoption claim ([Metaflow](https://metaflow.org/)). | It showed that the minimum ML platform need not have its own general UI or own the underlying runtime; a library and stable execution contract may be enough. |
| `2020-03-16` | Spotify; Backstage; autonomous engineering teams | Spotify open-sourced the framework behind an internal portal that had begun as a basic service catalogue and gained adoption voluntarily. Its catalogue treats repository YAML as the source of truth for ownership and metadata ([Spotify history](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2021/03/happy-birthday-backstage-spotifys-biggest-open-source-project-grows-up-fast), [Backstage catalogue docs](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/)). | Backstage became a CNCF project. Spotify’s internal instance grew far beyond the initial catalogue, while the open-source release was intentionally a framework rather than Spotify’s finished portal ([CNCF](https://www.cncf.io/blog/2022/03/15/backstage-project-joins-the-cncf-incubator/)). | A small metadata contract such as an agent descriptor can be consumed by an existing portal. The portal is optional to the contract, and plugins can add UI only for users who need it. |
| `2022-07-12` | Government Digital Service; GOV.UK PaaS; public-sector service teams; Cloud Foundry | GDS announced that a successful shared PaaS would be retired because public-cloud capabilities and departmental skills had improved, growth lagged other platforms, and modernization required major investment. | Decommissioning proceeded over an announced `18`-month window. GDS explicitly kept options open from doing nothing, through reusable configuration components, to a redesigned PaaS ([GDS decision](https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2022/07/12/why-weve-decided-to-decommission-gov-uk-paas-platform-as-a-service/)). | This is unusually clear evidence that “successful” and “still worth owning” are different tests. Subtraction can be the correct platform-product decision when the ecosystem absorbs the capability. |
| `2024-11-25` | Anthropic; MCP clients and servers; AI-tool developers | Anthropic released a protocol and SDKs to replace one-off AI connectors with a shared two-way contract. | MCP is still evolving; authorization and security guidance have expanded substantially since launch ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/model-context-protocol), [MCP security guidance](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/docs/tutorials/security/security_best_practices)). | The immediate precedent for a “smallest AI platform” is therefore a standard consumed by existing hosts, not necessarily a separate agent runtime or portal. |

## Failed attempts at the same thing

These cases include both abandoned systems and operating models that proved too weak. The point is not that every named platform “failed”; it is to identify the failure boundary that forced scope to change.

| Date | Attempt | What killed or constrained it | Are those factors still present? |
|---|---|---|---|
| `2017-09-05` | **Underpowered: Uber’s pre-Michelangelo arrangement.** Data scientists used varied local tools while engineering teams built one-off production systems. | There was no standard route for reproducible pipelines, large-scale training, experiment comparison, or model serving. The result depended on a small number of specialists and could not support repeated production use ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/michelangelo-machine-learning-platform/)). | Yes. Local agent prototypes can succeed while deployment, identity, company data, evaluation, and observability remain bespoke. This precedent supports adding a shared capability only after those gaps recur. |
| `2018-05-17` | **Underpowered: Netflix’s partial dev/ops ownership model.** Developers could deploy and handle some incidents, but ops specialists retained parts of release and production ownership. | Partial responsibility left ambiguous gaps, lossy transfers, and specialists who could not prioritize automation; release cycles and incident feedback remained slow ([Netflix](https://netflixtechblog.com/full-cycle-developers-at-netflix-a08c31f83249)). | Yes. “Teams own agents” is not sufficient if platform, security, or operations still retain opaque approval and incident steps. Ownership must include the interfaces and feedback loops, not only a policy statement. |
| `2018-06-08` | **Overbroad replacement: Google Omega as a clean successor to Borg.** Google planned a principled rewrite supporting the same use cases. | Borg kept evolving, improvement of Borg was easier than estimated, Omega’s benefits were overestimated, and migrating millions of configuration lines across thousands of services would have required years of dual operation. Google folded useful Omega ideas back into Borg and used others in Kubernetes instead ([Google SRE](https://sre.google/workbook/simplicity/)). | Absolutely. A new AI platform competes with the future version of Backstage, identity, observability, data, and cloud systems—not their current defects. Wide contracts and hidden downstream dependencies make “clean” replacement expensive. |
| `2022-07-12` | **No longer economical: GOV.UK PaaS.** The platform was reliable and useful, but renewal required a significant architectural investment. | Public clouds reduced entry barriers, departments built internal cloud skill, demand growth lagged, and an official live assessment had warned that recharge could never fully cover cost and that continued central funding was a longevity risk ([GDS decision](https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2022/07/12/why-weve-decided-to-decommission-gov-uk-paas-platform-as-a-service/), [live assessment](https://www.gov.uk/service-standard-reports/gov-dot-uk-platform-as-a-service-paas-live-assessment)). | Yes. Model providers, cloud services, protocols, and existing internal platforms are improving quickly. A dedicated capability can be correct now and still deserve retirement once suppliers or teams absorb it. |

Two broad lessons follow. First, “contracts only” is underpowered when nobody owns conformance, freshness, and migration: Backstage itself includes ingestion loops, validation, unprocessed-entity diagnostics, and owner-maintained metadata rather than treating YAML as self-executing documentation ([Backstage catalogue](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/), [catalogue API](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/software-catalog-api/)). Empirical software-architecture research likewise finds implementations diverge from intended architecture and that teams invest too little in keeping documentation current; a contract without automated checks should therefore be treated as a hypothesis, not a platform outcome ([survey and interviews across 37 countries](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0950584921001580)).

Second, broad platforms fail differently: they solve a real common need, then accumulate a wide API, mandatory migrations, fixed operating cost, and a constituency that makes removal hard. Google’s Omega account and the GOV.UK PaaS retirement are valuable precisely because they document subtraction and reuse of the surviving ideas instead of presenting replacement as an engineering defeat.

## Recurring cast

This section is supported, so it is produced despite being Optional in the research plan. Few named individuals recur across both the chapter and the cases; the recurring cast is institutional and role-based.

- **Backstage / Spotify** is the direct bridge to the present inquiry. Its repository-owned entity descriptors provide the concrete precedent for an `Agent` metadata kind, while its plugin architecture allows the UI and enforcement surface to grow independently of that contract ([descriptor format](https://backstage.io/docs/next/features/software-catalog/descriptor-format/)).
- **Platform teams** recur at Netflix, Uber, Google, Spotify, GDS, and in the chapter. Their durable role is not to centralize everything; it is to recognize repeated work, supply a supported default, observe adoption and outcomes, and own migration or retirement.
- **Stream-aligned or application teams** are the consumers and the source of local knowledge. Netflix and Team Topologies both make their independence the constraint on platform design; DORA’s reported stability and throughput trade-off reinforces that independence cannot be assumed after adoption ([Netflix](https://netflixtechblog.com/full-cycle-developers-at-netflix-a08c31f83249), [Team Topologies](https://teamtopologies.com/s/Organization-Dynamics-with-Team-Topologies-Mini-book-MB80.pdf), [DORA](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/)).
- **Existing infrastructure and standards bodies** repeatedly absorb successful ideas: Cloud Foundry adopted buildpacks, CNCF became Backstage’s neutral home, and MCP builds its authorization on existing OAuth standards. The recurring historical move is compositional reuse, not a vertically complete new platform.
- **Finance, security, compliance, and operations** appear whenever local autonomy reaches production. Their demand is usually for evidence, attribution, bounds, and incident response—not necessarily for a dedicated UI. SLOs, metadata, policy checks, and traces can satisfy part of this need as contracts consumed by systems they already use.

## What’s actually new this time

The defensible conclusion is that most proposed AI-platform capabilities are established platform work with new nouns. A smaller set is genuinely AI-specific because the execution semantics have changed.

| Capability | Classification | Historical basis or new requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Service ownership, catalogue, documentation, templates, API discovery | **Old pattern, new branding** | Backstage already models repository-owned metadata, APIs, owners, systems, and templates; adding an agent kind does not itself require an AI platform ([catalogue](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/), [templates](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-templates/)). |
| Identity, secrets, deployment, networking, availability, incident response | **Old pattern, new consumer** | These are the same platform and SRE responsibilities documented by Netflix and Google. Agents may consume them differently, but the organizational capability is established ([Netflix paved road](https://netflixtechblog.com/how-we-build-code-at-netflix-c5d9bd727f15), [Google SRE](https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/)). |
| Cost allocation, quotas, vendor routing, and showback | **Mostly old pattern** | Cloud FinOps and API gateway concerns already require attribution and policy. Token-based pricing and rapid model substitution change the unit and volatility, not the underlying platform responsibility. |
| Probabilistic task evaluation and behavioural regression | **Genuinely AI-specific floor** | Conventional health checks cannot establish whether a nondeterministic model still completes a task acceptably. Google’s ML-debt research identifies changing data, feedback loops, and system-level anti-patterns that ordinary software practices do not fully cover ([Sculley et al.](https://research.google/pubs/hidden-technical-debt-in-machine-learning-systems/)). |
| Prompt, model, retrieval source, tool, and evaluation-set provenance | **Genuinely AI-specific composition** | Versioning exists in software and ML, but an agent’s behaviour depends on a combined, partly external context whose exact reconstruction may require recording all of these elements. NIST’s Generative AI Profile treats measurement, evaluation, provenance, and lifecycle risk as cross-cutting GenAI concerns ([NIST AI 600-1](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence)). |
| Model-mediated tool choice and delegated authority | **Genuinely agent-specific** | An LLM may select and sequence tools using untrusted context. OWASP identifies excessive agency and indirect prompt injection as routes to damaging actions, while MCP now specifies resource-bound tokens, least privilege, and per-call mediation ([OWASP excessive agency](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/2_0_vulns/LLM06_ExcessiveAgency.html), [MCP authorization](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/draft/basic/authorization)). |
| A dedicated web interface for agent builders | **Not inherently new or necessary** | Netflix deliberately integrated Metaflow with notebooks rather than providing a universal UI; Backstage can render contract metadata and actions; CLIs and repository templates can serve engineers. A UI becomes necessary only for a user job that those interfaces cannot satisfy. |
| Running agents as a shared runtime | **Contingent, not a universal floor** | Michelangelo and TFX show when shared runtimes pay off: repeated large-scale training, serving, and monitoring needs. Metaflow shows a thinner library-over-existing-infrastructure alternative. The same fork applies to agents: runtime ownership follows proven common execution constraints, not the “agent” label. |
| Open tool/context protocol | **New standard atop an old pattern** | MCP is AI-specific in payload and consumer, but historically resembles build, API, and metadata contracts: it standardizes an integration seam so hosts and runtimes can remain plural ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/model-context-protocol)). |

The novelty test therefore rejects both extremes. “Nothing is new; reuse ordinary DevOps” ignores probabilistic evaluation, context provenance, prompt injection, and delegated tool authority. “Everything needs an AI platform” relabels catalogues, identity, deployment, observability, cost attribution, and ownership that mature organizations already have.

## What an experienced observer would expect next

1. **Start with a contract inside the existing developer platform.** The modal first move is an agent descriptor beside the code—owner, purpose, risk class, model/provider, tools, data classes, evaluation reference, deployment/runtime link, and cost attribution—ingested by Backstage. Heroku’s application contract, Backstage’s repository YAML, and MCP’s integration standard all show that a narrow contract can coordinate several downstream tools without introducing a new UI or runtime ([Heroku](https://www.heroku.com/blog/twelve-factor-apps/), [Backstage](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/), [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/model-context-protocol)).

2. **Add validation before adding orchestration.** Metadata will become stale or inconsistent if it is only documentation. Backstage already exposes dry-run validation and unprocessed-entity diagnostics, and the architecture-drift evidence predicts divergence without maintenance. An experienced platform team would therefore add schema validation, ownership checks, and CI conformance before building a new control plane ([Backstage API](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/software-catalog-api/), [architecture/source-code study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0950584921001580)).

3. **Earn adoption with one high-frequency path.** Netflix’s paved road and Metaflow both succeeded by preserving escape hatches and making common operations conspicuously easier. The likely first managed capability is not “run every agent”; it is one recurring bottleneck such as approved model access, deployment, or task evaluation, with local alternatives remaining possible at local cost ([Netflix paved road](https://netflixtechblog.com/how-we-build-code-at-netflix-c5d9bd727f15), [Metaflow](https://netflixtechblog.com/open-sourcing-metaflow-a-human-centric-framework-for-data-science-fa72e04a5d9)).

4. **Scope will grow at the risk boundary, not evenly.** Low-risk experiments may need only metadata and model access. Tool-using or sensitive agents will pull in authorization mediation, traceability, evaluation gates, incident hooks, and evidence retention because model-mediated actions create new failure paths ([MCP client guidance](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/docs/develop/clients/client-best-practices), [OWASP](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/2_0_vulns/LLM06_ExcessiveAgency.html), [NIST](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence)).

5. **The first broad redesign proposal should be challenged against extension and subtraction.** Google’s Omega lesson is to compare a new system with the improved future incumbent, including migration and dual-running cost. The likely better question is whether Backstage, identity, gateways, observability, and FinOps can each absorb one AI-specific field or check ([Google SRE](https://sre.google/workbook/simplicity/)).

6. **Measure both work removed and work displaced.** DORA’s mixed platform result predicts that self-service may raise perceived productivity while harming delivery stability or throughput. The platform should track waiting time, successful task completion, exceptions, bypass, support load, incident outcomes, audit effort, and cost per successful outcome—not adoption alone ([DORA](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/)).

7. **Plan retirement at creation.** GOV.UK PaaS shows a reliable, valuable platform can still be the wrong continuing investment once providers and teams catch up. Each capability should ship with owners, consumers, a replacement seam, usage telemetry, migration policy, and a retirement trigger. The smallest platform is not merely small on launch; it remains possible to shrink ([GDS](https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2022/07/12/why-weve-decided-to-decommission-gov-uk-paas-platform-as-a-service/), [GOV.UK API retirement guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/defining-an-api-management-strategy)).
