# Stakeholders, money, and power

## Working answer

The strongest evidence does not support “lack of an AI platform” as a universal business problem. It supports a narrower diagnosis: once several teams are putting probabilistic systems into production, somebody must repeatedly coordinate model and data access, deployment, evaluation, monitoring, cost attribution, security, and compliance. A shared platform can turn that repeated work into common services and enforceable controls. It cannot supply a worthwhile use case, redesign a workflow, create skills, or make an organization capable of measuring value.

This distinction matters because the people who feel the coordination problem are not always the people who buy the solution. Application teams experience integration and approval friction; security, legal, and finance experience loss of visibility; executives experience a gap between AI activity and value; platform vendors are paid when all four problems are described as one platform-shaped problem. The UK government’s representative business survey is a useful check: 71% of businesses cited no identified need as a barrier, while 60% cited limited skills; among current adopters, 40% said lack of tools or platforms had previously been a barrier ([DSIT, updated 2026-02-13](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-adoption-research/ai-adoption-research)). A platform addresses the third problem directly, the second only partly, and the first not at all.

## By the numbers

The figures answer different questions and should not be merged into one adoption rate. OECD and Eurostat use official enterprise surveys; Stack Overflow and CNCF survey technical communities; KPMG and IBM survey large-enterprise executives and sell AI-related services; prices are mutable list prices.

### Adoption and who actually has the problem

1. Across OECD countries with available data, 20.2% of firms reported AI use in the 2025 reporting year, versus 14.2% in 2024 and 8.7% in 2023; the large/small-firm split was 52.0% versus 17.4% ([OECD, 2026-01-28](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/announcements/2026/01/ai-use-by-individuals-surges-across-the-oecd-as-adoption-by-firms-continues-to-expand.html)).
2. Eurostat found 20.0% of EU enterprises with at least ten workers used AI in the 2025 reporting year; adoption ranged from 42.0% in Denmark to 5.2% in Romania ([Eurostat, 2025-12-11](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20251211-2)). The geographical and firm-size gaps imply that platform economics cannot be assumed from frontier-company case studies.
3. In the UK’s representative 3,500-business survey, 16% used at least one AI technology and 80% neither used nor planned to use one. The top cited barriers were no identified need (71%) and limited skills (60%); lack of tools/platforms was cited by 40% of current adopters and 54% of intended adopters ([DSIT, updated 2026-02-13](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-adoption-research/ai-adoption-research)).
4. The UK Business Data Survey found only 21% of AI-using businesses had AI tools integrated into existing business systems. Integrated users had more developed data practices, but the survey is observational and does not show that integration caused those practices ([DSIT, 2026-06-18](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/uk-business-data-survey-2026/uk-business-data-survey-2026)).

### Architecture and organization

5. Among 364 CNCF/SlashData respondents integrating AI workflows, 35% used separate experimentation with shared production deployment, 19% had a dedicated AI platform, 17% extended an existing developer platform, 18% primarily relied on vendor AI platforms, and 5% left infrastructure to AI teams without platform support ([CNCF, 2026-03-24](https://www.cncf.io/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Q1-2026-CNCF-Technology-Radar-Report.pdf)). The most common answer was hybrid, not monolithic.
6. In the same survey, 41% of 422 respondents said multiple DevOps/SRE/infrastructure teams collaboratively provided platform capabilities, while 28% reported a platform team. This is evidence that the problem is often ownership and coordination before it is product selection ([CNCF, 2026-03-24](https://www.cncf.io/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Q1-2026-CNCF-Technology-Radar-Report.pdf)).
7. A study combining a systematic review with interviews of 12 ML practitioners found four challenge classes—organizational, technical, operational, and business—across eleven themes. Its small qualitative sample supports a taxonomy, not a prevalence estimate ([Amrit and Kolar, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jik.2024.100637)).
8. A preprint analysing more than 8,000 G2 reviews found seven of nine MLOps practices positively associated with user satisfaction, but review selection, platform-vendor context, and observational design prevent a causal claim about business value ([Pasch, 2025-10-11](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09968)).

### Outcomes, trust, and control

9. Stack Overflow received more than 49,000 responses across 177 countries. Of respondents answering its AI questions, 84% used or planned to use AI tools, yet 46% distrusted output accuracy versus 33% who trusted it; 66% cited “almost right” answers as a frustration ([Stack Overflow, 2025-07-29](https://stackoverflow.co/company/press/archive/stack-overflow-2025-developer-survey/); [survey detail](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai)). A platform therefore needs verification and escalation, not merely access.
10. Among Stack Overflow agent users, about 70% reported task-time reduction and 69% productivity improvement, but only 17% reported better team collaboration. Personal acceleration and organizational coordination are different outcomes ([Stack Overflow, 2025 Developer Survey](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai)).
11. DORA’s nearly 5,000-person study describes AI as an amplifier of strong or weak organizational conditions. Its earlier 2025.2 analysis estimated that each 25% increase in AI adoption was associated with 1.5% lower delivery throughput and 7.2% lower delivery stability, despite perceived individual benefits; DORA explicitly hypothesised larger change batches and insufficient testing as mechanisms ([DORA, 2025](https://research.google/pubs/dora-2025-state-of-ai-assisted-software-development-report/); [DORA impact report v2025.2](https://dora.dev/research/ai/gen-ai-report/dora-impact-of-generative-ai-in-software-development.pdf)).
12. IBM’s vendor-sponsored survey of 2,000 technology executives found 77% said adoption was outpacing governance, 85% lacked full real-time AI-spend visibility, and respondents reported an average of 54 agent incidents in the preceding year. These are self-reports from decision-makers, not independently audited incident records ([IBM, 2026-06-08](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-06-08-new-ibm-study-finds-cios-and-ctos-face-growing-ai-control-gap-as-enterprise-deployment-scales?asPDF=1&lnk=hpln1id)).

### Money and physical scale

13. KPMG’s survey of 2,110 senior leaders at large organizations reported 95% had an AI strategy, 64% saw “meaningful business value,” 39% were scaling organization-wide, but only 8% had established ROI; respondents planned an average US$186 million of investment over the following twelve months ([KPMG, 2026-03-31](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmgsites/xx/pdf/2026/04/global-ai-pulse.pdf.coredownload.pdf)). “Meaningful value” and “established ROI” are not equivalent.
14. Current list prices expose why a platform creates a FinOps problem of its own: AWS charges US$0.21 per completed human evaluation task and US$1 per 1,000 intelligent-routing requests, on top of model and related-service consumption ([AWS Bedrock pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/bedrock/pricing/)); Google lists Gemini 2.5 Pro at US$1.25 per million input tokens and US$10 per million output tokens at up to 200,000 input tokens, with separate grounding charges ([Google Vertex AI pricing](https://cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/pricing)).
15. Alphabet reported US$91.4 billion in 2025 capital expenditure, mainly technical infrastructure, and US$149.1 billion in purchase commitments and other obligations at 2025-12-31, mostly short-term technical infrastructure and inventory orders ([Alphabet 2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1652044/000165204426000018/goog-20251231.htm)). The platform business transfers customer demand upstream into chips, data centres, networks, and energy.
16. The IEA estimated global data-centre electricity use at 485 TWh in the 2025 reporting year and projects about 950 TWh in 2030; AI-focused data-centre consumption rose 50% in 2025 and is projected to triple by 2030 ([IEA, 2026-04-16](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)). The IEA also says capital expenditure by five large technology companies exceeded US$400 billion in 2025, showing that upstream scale advantages are difficult for smaller entrants to reproduce.

## Power and authority map

Centralization is not neutral: it converts technical defaults into organizational policy. The key question is not only “who owns the platform?” but “who can say no, grant an exception, see the evidence, and move the workload?”

| Pivotal decision | Formal decider | Required approval sequence | Explicit veto holders | Soft influence without formal authority | Reversal conditions | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Which models and providers are allowed? | CIO/CTO or delegated AI governance body, with procurement and security authority | Product need → technical/evaluation review → security/privacy review → procurement/contract approval → catalogue publication | CISO for unacceptable security exposure; DPO/privacy counsel for unlawful processing; procurement for unacceptable terms; regulated risk owner where applicable | Platform engineers, model vendors, cloud account teams, finance, and application teams shape defaults and evidence | New incident, model change, failed evaluation, contract change, sanctions/export restriction, or successful portability review | [FTC, 2025-01-17](https://search.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-issues-staff-report-ai-partnerships-investments-study); [NIST AI RMF](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/) |

## Under-recognised winners and losers

### Winners

- **FinOps and central risk functions.** A platform makes otherwise scattered calls and permissions legible. IBM’s finding that 85% of surveyed technology leaders lacked real-time spend visibility and AWS’s fine-grained attribution options show why cost and control functions may be the most immediate beneficiaries—even before product ROI appears ([IBM, 2026-06-08](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-06-08-new-ibm-study-finds-cios-and-ctos-face-growing-ai-control-gap-as-enterprise-deployment-scales?asPDF=1&lnk=hpln1id); [AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/cost-management.html)).
- **Existing developer-platform and observability teams.** The category is not necessarily won by a separate AI organization. CNCF found only 19% using a dedicated AI platform, while Stack Overflow found agent builders reusing Grafana/Prometheus and Sentry. Existing teams can extend their mandate and budget rather than yield to a new silo ([CNCF](https://www.cncf.io/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Q1-2026-CNCF-Technology-Radar-Report.pdf); [Stack Overflow](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai)).
- **Integrated cloud vendors and upstream suppliers.** They earn across several meters—compute, model access, data, retrieval, evaluation, networking—and can use integration to increase switching costs. FTC, CMA, OECD, and Alphabet’s filing all point to power accumulating across layers rather than in the user-facing platform alone.
- **Audit, evaluation, identity, and policy components.** Regulation and low developer trust make these formerly peripheral functions part of the production path. The platform’s durable value may lie more in proving and constraining behavior than in generating output.
- **Large, digitally mature enterprises.** Official OECD, Eurostat, and UK surveys consistently show much higher adoption among large firms. Shared-platform fixed costs are easier to spread across many teams and use cases, while existing data, risk, and procurement functions can operate the controls.

### Losers

- **Small firms asked to buy enterprise coordination before they have enterprise scale.** OECD reports 52.0% AI adoption in large firms versus 17.4% in small firms; DSIT found “no identified need” was the dominant barrier. A platform can become premature overhead.
- **Product teams whose local knowledge is overruled by central defaults.** Allow-lists, uniform evaluations, and approval queues reduce duplicated risk but can also remove the discretion needed to test a better model or architecture. The CNCF hybrid plurality is evidence that organizations preserve separate experimentation for a reason.
- **Smaller model and infrastructure providers outside a preferred cloud’s catalogue.** FTC and OECD warn that partnerships, bundled access, compute concentration, and switching costs may shape downstream competition before end customers choose.
- **Workers, data labourers, and affected people whose judgment supplies the system but whose voice is not represented in platform dashboards.** The ILO identifies data labour and worker autonomy as overlooked. Their work may be measured as a platform input or incident-reduction target rather than as an interest with decision rights.
- **Communities hosting compute and power infrastructure.** The platform abstracts physical location from developers, but IEA data show large, geographically concentrated electricity demand. Benefits flow globally while reliability, land, water, and affordability effects are local.
- **Organizations that mistake control for capability.** A clean inventory and a green compliance dashboard can make a weak use case easier to scale. DORA’s amplifier result and KPMG’s ROI gap both warn that operational maturity magnifies the underlying organization; it does not choose the right problem.

## The silence

Producing this optional section because the evidence itself reveals systematic absences.

1. **Workers and affected people are mostly missing from buyer-side evidence.** The DSIT adoption survey interviewed the person responsible for technology and explicitly says it cannot see shadow AI; KPMG and IBM survey senior leaders; CNCF and Stack Overflow survey technical practitioners. Those are valuable perspectives, but none is a representative survey of the employees whose work is reorganized or people subject to AI-mediated decisions. The ILO’s call for employer–worker social dialogue is therefore not an accessory: it identifies a missing authority in platform governance ([ILO/NASK, 2025-05-20](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/one-four-jobs-risk-being-transformed-genai-new-ilo%E2%80%93nask-global-index-shows)).
2. **Non-adopters and SMEs are quiet in platform communities.** Platform forums condition participation on already having a scaling problem. Official data show the median business is elsewhere: in the UK survey, 80% neither used nor planned to use AI, and 71% cited no need. The risk is sampling on adopters and then universalizing their coordination problem.
3. **Independent, causal platform ROI is largely absent.** Academic work defines MLOps and catalogs challenges; a preprint links practices to review satisfaction; vendor surveys correlate reported governance maturity with outcomes. I found no neutral study that randomly or quasi-experimentally compares a dedicated AI platform with an extended developer platform and a composable stack on fully loaded cost, lead time, incident rate, switching cost, and business value. This absence should cap confidence in any universal buy/build threshold.
4. **Local infrastructure externalities are abstracted out of software evidence.** Pricing pages meter tokens and requests; they do not show the marginal grid, water, or land effect at the serving location. IEA models the aggregate and regional physical burden, but customer platform dashboards rarely expose it.
5. **An official UK source contradicts itself.** The 2026 UK Business Data Survey’s executive summary says 17% of AI-using businesses reported “no policy in place,” while its detailed section and Figure 18 say 17% had a policy or guidelines (5% formal and 12% informal). The detailed base is 1,870 businesses. This dossier does not treat the 17% as a settled prevalence claim without clarification from DSIT ([DSIT, 2026-06-18](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/uk-business-data-survey-2026/uk-business-data-survey-2026)).

## Bottom line

An AI platform is most defensible when it solves a repeated **coordination-and-control** problem across multiple production teams: common access, evidence, operations, risk, and cost. The evidence is much weaker for a dedicated AI platform when the organization has few use cases, weak data and delivery practices, no agreed business owner, or an existing developer platform that can be extended. The platform’s central bargain is therefore:

> Give up some local tool and model agency in exchange for lower repeated integration cost, shared operational evidence, and organizational visibility.

Whether that bargain is good depends on scale, risk, existing platform maturity, and exit options. A useful falsifier is simple: if the organization cannot name duplicated lifecycle work, an accountable owner, a measurable outcome, and a cheaper shared path, the “AI platform problem” may be a vendor category looking for a budget.
